  - block: markdown
    content:
      title: Background
      text: |
        In high-beta plasmas, electromagnetic turbulence driven by microtearing modes is considered to be one of the causes of anomalous electron heat transport. Several mechanisms have been proposed for the nonlinear saturation of this turbulence. Among them, the role of zonal magnetic fields spontaneously generated by the turbulence remains controversial: some studies have reported that they play an important role, while others have reported that they do not.

        **Background temperature gradient**

        We consider the x–y plane perpendicular to a strong background magnetic field B<sub>0</sub> (in the z direction), in a slab geometry where the electron temperature varies in the x direction and decreases toward positive x (figure 1). This background electron temperature gradient is the energy source that destabilizes the microtearing mode. All simulation results shown below are distributions in this x–y plane.

        <img src="/files/self_organization/background_gradient_en.svg" alt="Schematic of the background electron temperature gradient and the simulation plane" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:640px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Figure 1: (a) Background electron temperature profile, which decreases toward positive x. (b) The x–y plane used in the simulations. The color represents the background temperature (red: hot, blue: cold), and the background magnetic field points out of the page.</small>

        **What is magnetic shear?**

        Magnetic shear refers to a change in the direction of the magnetic field with position. In slab geometry, it is represented by adding a y component B<sub>y</sub>(x), proportional to x, to the main field B<sub>0</sub> in the z direction (figure 2(b)). The tilt of the field lines then varies with x, and magnetic islands tend to form near the position where B<sub>y</sub> = 0, which corresponds to a rational surface. Without B<sub>y</sub>, the field lines point in the same direction at every position (figure 2(a)).

        <img src="/files/self_organization/magnetic_shear_en.svg" alt="Schematic of magnetic shear" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:640px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Figure 2: Schematic of magnetic shear. Each plane represents a y–z plane at fixed x, and the blue arrows indicate the direction of the field lines. (a) Without magnetic shear. (b) With magnetic shear.</small>

        **Aim of this study**

        Regions of weak or nearly zero magnetic shear are realized in stellarators and in advanced tokamak scenarios. In this study, we perform nonlinear simulations with the gyrokinetic code GS2 in the simplest slab geometry without equilibrium magnetic shear, and investigate how magnetic islands and zonal magnetic fields self-organize in microtearing turbulence. Since magnetic shear acts only to stabilize the microtearing mode, this configuration retains its destabilization mechanism, i.e., the time-dependent thermal force.

      title: Picture of the Self-Organization
      text: |
        **Electron temperature fluctuations carry most of the energy**

        The time evolution of the gyrokinetic free energy shows that most of it is contained in the electron particle energy K<sub>e</sub>, in particular in isotropic electron temperature fluctuations, whereas the magnetic energy M<sub>⊥</sub> remains much smaller (figure 3). Unlike conventional tearing instabilities, which release energy stored in the equilibrium magnetic field, microtearing modes are driven by the electron temperature gradient. Nevertheless, the magnetic field structure plays a crucial role in the nonlinear evolution of the turbulence.

        <img src="/files/self_organization/energy_evolution.png" alt="Time evolution of the gyrokinetic free energy" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:520px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Figure 3: Time evolution of the gyrokinetic free energy W and its components. Most of W is carried by the electron particle energy K<sub>e</sub>, almost all of which is the energy of isotropic electron temperature fluctuations, K<sub>e</sub><sup>T</sup>. The magnetic energy M<sub>⊥</sub> (purple) is small.</small>

        **Magnetic islands appear without magnetic shear**

        Figure 4 shows snapshots of the electron temperature fluctuation power and the magnetic field lines (contours of the vector potential A<sub>∥</sub>). The magnetic field is initially turbulent (a), and chains of magnetic islands aligned in the y direction are subsequently formed (b). These islands are formed even though the equilibrium has no magnetic shear. The island width then gradually decreases (c), and the zonal field structure becomes dominant (d). The temperature fluctuations remain localized along the field lines, indicating a strong coupling between the magnetic perturbations and the electron temperature fluctuations.

        <img src="/files/self_organization/temperature_power_snapshots.png" alt="Snapshots of the electron temperature fluctuation power and the magnetic field lines" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:520px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Figure 4: Snapshots of the electron temperature fluctuation power (color) and contours of A<sub>∥</sub> (magnetic field lines). Time is normalized by the electron diamagnetic drift frequency.</small>

        **Zonal fields act as an effective magnetic shear**

        When A<sub>∥</sub> is decomposed into its zonal component (uniform in the y direction) and non-zonal components, the island chains disappear once the zonal component is removed (figure 5). The zonal magnetic fields self-generated by the turbulence thus act as an effective magnetic shear, and magnetic islands are formed by their superposition with the magnetic perturbations. Indeed, the O-points of the islands are located near the positions where the zonal magnetic field ⟨B<sub>y</sub>⟩ vanishes (figure 6).

        <img src="/files/self_organization/apar_zonal_nonzonal.png" alt="Zonal and non-zonal components of A∥" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:600px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Figure 5: (a) Zonal and (b) non-zonal components of A<sub>∥</sub> during island formation. The island chains do not appear in the non-zonal components alone.</small>

        <img src="/files/self_organization/zonal_field_profile.png" alt="Profile of the zonal magnetic field in the x direction" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:440px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Figure 6: Profiles of the zonal magnetic field ⟨B<sub>y</sub>⟩ in the x direction at different times. The structure develops toward larger scales with time.</small>

        To identify the source of the zonal magnetic fields, we analyze the power balance of A<sub>∥</sub> based on the generalized Ohm's law. It is shown that the zonal fields are generated mainly by the nonlinear interaction between the magnetic perturbations and the electron temperature fluctuations.

        **The electron temperature profile is flattened within the islands**

        Furthermore, we find that within the self-generated islands, the electron temperature fluctuations spontaneously organize to produce a gradient that nearly cancels the background temperature gradient (animation). This flattening weakens the local temperature-gradient drive of the microtearing instability and is therefore expected to promote turbulence saturation. The same mechanism may operate with and without equilibrium magnetic shear.

        <img src="/files/self_organization/self_organization.gif" alt="Time evolution of the electron temperature fluctuation and the magnetic field lines" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:440px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Animation: Time evolution of the electron temperature fluctuation (color) and the magnetic field lines (contours). Red and blue denote regions hotter and colder than the background, respectively. Within the islands, the temperature fluctuations are distributed so as to cancel the background temperature gradient.</small>

        **The self-organization cycle**

        The overall process is summarized in figure 7. The temperature gradient destabilizes the microtearing mode, and the turbulence generates both zonal magnetic fields and magnetic perturbations. Their superposition forms magnetic islands, and the flattening of the temperature within the islands weakens the drive of the instability.

        <img src="/files/self_organization/flowchart.png" alt="Schematic diagram of the self-organization process" style="display:block; width:100%; max-width:560px; height:auto; margin:0 auto;">

        <small>Figure 7: Schematic diagram of the self-organization process driven by microtearing modes in the absence of magnetic shear.</small>

  - block: markdown
    content:
      title: Future Directions
      text: |
        We speculate that the role of zonal magnetic fields depends on the strength of magnetic shear. In weak-magnetic-shear regimes, self-generated zonal fields may provide an effective magnetic shear and play an important role in the nonlinear evolution, whereas such self-organization may be suppressed under sufficiently strong magnetic shear. A systematic investigation of the relationship between magnetic shear and zonal field generation, as well as of the subsequent evolution toward turbulence saturation, is left as future work. The results may also have implications for nearly-zero-shear regions in the Wendelstein 7-X stellarator and for the advanced tokamak scenarios planned for JT-60SA.
